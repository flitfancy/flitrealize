# 5.1 Prototype bring-up, testing, and revision

Read this reference when hardware is available for first power, functional validation, fault isolation, or revision decisions.

Tests come from current requirements, design assumptions, and prototype items left by the schematic stage. Prove what this prototype needs without creating a universal laboratory validation program.

## Prepare for power

Confirm the current:

- board revision and actual assembly state;
- input source, polarity, and allowed range;
- major rails and expected voltages;
- expected startup current and conservative current limit;
- default states of outputs, enables, and protection;
- important test points;
- functions and assumptions to verify;
- stop conditions.

Current limits and stop conditions come from this design. When an exact value is unavailable, begin with a conservative value that can support safe startup and adjust as evidence is gained.

A meter and current-limited supply usually cover basic bring-up. Use an oscilloscope, logic analyzer, electronic load, thermal camera, or automated logging only when the current question benefits from it.

## Inspect the unpowered prototype

Before connecting power, check:

- part identity, orientation, and polarity;
- connector and jumper state;
- bridges, poor joints, debris, and visible damage;
- rework and unpopulated parts;
- major rails for an obvious short to ground;
- clear identification of input, output, and battery terminals.

Measure only rails that matter to this design. Do not create resistance records for every net without a decision use.

Correct an obvious assembly error, short, or polarity problem before applying power.

## First power

Use an observable sequence with clear stop points:

1. Disconnect unnecessary external loads and high-energy actuators.
2. Set the correct input voltage and a conservative current limit.
3. Apply input and observe current.
4. Measure major rails.
5. Check for abnormal heat, smell, sound, or oscillation.
6. Confirm reset, enable, and output defaults.
7. Connect or enable remaining blocks one at a time.
8. Observe current, voltage, and temperature after each added load.

Stop when:

- input current is clearly above expectation;
- a rail voltage or polarity is wrong;
- a part heats rapidly;
- protection cycles repeatedly;
- unexplained sustained oscillation, sound, smell, or other behavior appears.

Preserve the observation and measurement before deciding whether the cause is assembly, circuit, firmware, or test setup.

## Validate functions from requirements

After basic power is correct, test the actual product functions. As applicable, check:

- input and output range;
- interface communication;
- startup, reset, and enable;
- sensors, actuators, and loads;
- operating modes;
- accuracy, ripple, and temperature rise;
- load steps and startup transients;
- normal power cycles and recovery;
- protection or fault behavior explicitly required by the design.

Test normal operation before boundaries and faults. Test only faults that the current design can safely tolerate and actually needs to prove.

Choose duration and repetition from the failure timescale. Do not start long-duration logging without a long-term stability claim.

## Record the minimum useful result

Use a compact record such as:

```text
Test | Condition | Expected | Measured | Result | Evidence
```

Also record board revision, assembly or rework state, input condition, firmware version, and instrument settings when they affect reproduction. Routine passing items do not need long reports. Preserve enough measurement, waveform, image, or log to reproduce an anomaly.

Classify results plainly:

- met under the tested condition;
- usable with a known limit or remaining boundary test;
- disagrees with expectation and needs diagnosis;
- cannot be decided under the current condition.

One prototype test supports only the board, conditions, and function actually covered. It does not automatically establish batch or product behavior.

## Handle anomalies

First classify an anomaly as likely belonging to:

- assembly or soldering;
- schematic or part selection;
- PCB, return path, thermal, or mechanical design;
- firmware or configuration;
- test method or external condition.

When the cause is clear, correct it and retest. When one issue needs repeated measurements or experiments, enter CURIOUS_MODE only for that issue and read [0.2 Repeated-failure debugging](0.2-debug-loop.md).

Do not change several plausible causes in one experiment. Expand the review only when evidence shows that other blocks may be affected.

## Decide whether to revise

Revise from evidence and the actual product goal, for example when:

- a functional or safety requirement is not met;
- a part, pin, package, or connection is wrong;
- power, signal, thermal, or mechanical margin is insufficient;
- a real manufacturing, assembly, test, or service problem exists;
- a current limitation blocks the product goal.

Do not create a hardware revision for appearance alone, an irreproducible behavior, or a feature with no defined use.

For a proposed revision, record:

- the observed problem;
- supporting evidence;
- the design object to change;
- the measurable effect expected after the change;
- validation that must be repeated.

Return to the stage that owns the fact and update its artifact. Do not keep new design decisions only in a test log.

## Finish this stage

Be able to state:

- which core functions were verified on hardware;
- the tested conditions and limits;
- which issues require rework, another experiment, or revision;
- whether the next step is more testing, design change, or completion of the prototype goal.

Read [6.1 Productization and formal release](6.1-production-release.md) only when the user explicitly requests repeat production, batch PCBA, a formal version, or a release conclusion.
