# 2.1 Schematic design and Contract

Read this reference when requirements, architecture, and major parts are sufficiently stable to define schematic intent, check connectivity, or prepare for EDA.

This stage turns the design into accurate, inspectable connections. It does not reopen the entire product architecture.

## Inputs from design

Start with what the current task needs from the design stage:

- product goal and explicit non-goals;
- functional block diagram;
- power tree;
- external interfaces;
- important operating conditions;
- accepted parts and remaining candidate decisions;
- key calculations;
- questions intentionally left for prototype testing.

If an open question changes a functional block, power structure, major interface, or core part strategy, return to design rather than hiding it behind a local schematic change.

## Build the machine-readable Contract

Use `design/SCHEMATIC_CONTRACT.v1.json` for the portable schematic intent.

Its format is defined by:

```text
<skill>/schemas/schematic-contract.v1.schema.json
```

The Contract is the machine authority for generation, checking, and schematic comparison. It is independent of EDA and does not own canvas coordinates or display style.

Record as needed by the project:

- functional blocks;
- power domains and rails;
- component roles;
- manufacturer, exact MPN, suffix, and package for `exact` parts;
- value, required ratings, tolerance, package, and allowed range for `generic` parts;
- interface direction and voltage domain;
- semantic pins;
- pin-to-net connections;
- pullups, pulldowns, enables, and safe defaults;
- NC, DNC, thermal pads, and exposed pads that must connect;
- protection parts and fault-shutdown paths;
- test points;
- questions still assigned to prototype testing.

Do not add irrelevant fields for format completeness or copy discussion and rejected options into the Contract.

## Keep pin facts clear

Different layers own different facts:

```text
Manufacturer datasheet
    → native pinout and electrical facts

Schematic Contract
    → how this project uses those pins

EDA Provider binding
    → how the selected EDA symbol maps to those pins

EDA schematic readback
    → what was actually drawn
```

Use stable semantic names such as `VIN`, `GND`, `SCL`, `SDA`, `EN`, `SW`, and `FB` in the Contract.

An EDA Provider may map one semantic pin to one or more native symbol pins:

```json
{
  "bindings": {
    "easyedaPro": {
      "libraryUuid": "...",
      "deviceUuid": "...",
      "pinMap": {
        "VIN": ["1", "2"],
        "GND": ["3"]
      }
    }
  }
}
```

The Provider binding maps EDA identity. It cannot change the Contract's electrical design.

If the exact symbol, pin number, or mapping is ambiguous, pause EDA connection for that part and resolve the identity or pin relation. Do not infer it from a similar name, symbol position, or first search result.

## Limit pin reassignment

Pin mapping is fixed by default. Only MCUs, FPGAs, and other parts with confirmed configurable pins may be reconsidered after rough PCB placement.

Use only two policies:

- `fixed`: keep the current assignment;
- `unused_configurable_only`: move a signal only to an unused pin on the same device that is confirmed to support the required function.

Do not consider power, ground, reset, crystal, startup, debug, fixed analog, differential, NC, DNC, reserved, or already assigned pins.

The purpose is to remove an obvious crossing, detour, congestion point, or via. Do not search all permutations or perform chained swaps to create theoretical optimum routing. Keep the current assignment when there is no clearly better safe candidate.

After a real reassignment, update the Contract, schematic, and affected firmware interface together.

Until Contract v1 gains a dedicated field, record this policy in the component or interface `constraints` or `extensions`.

## Record early PCB intent

The schematic stage may record electrical conditions that the PCB must later implement, such as:

- net class;
- continuous and peak current;
- voltage and allowed drop;
- switching or sensitive analog status;
- differential, impedance, or length relationship;
- need for few vias, short routing, or continuous reference.

These facts guide PCB work but do not freeze final line width. Actual width, clearance, vias, and copper depend on the real stackup, copper weight, length, thermal path, and manufacturing capability.

## Derive a human-readable pin map

When pin-by-pin review helps, derive:

```text
design/PIN_NET_MAP.md
```

It may show:

- component;
- semantic pin;
- datasheet pin name;
- project role;
- connected net;
- power domain;
- safe default;
- required-connect, NC, or DNC status.

`PIN_NET_MAP.md` is a reading view, not a second source of connectivity. Update the Contract first, then regenerate or synchronize the view.

Do not require an extra overview when a small Contract is already easy to read.

## Review by function

### Power entry

Check:

- input range and polarity;
- connector rating;
- reverse input, surge, and ESD;
- input limiting or fuse behavior;
- connect, disconnect, and abnormal states;
- current return path.

### Regulators and rails

Check:

- input and output range;
- maximum and peak load;
- dropout, efficiency, loss, and temperature rise;
- inductors, capacitors, and feedback network;
- capacitor voltage rating and necessary derating;
- startup, soft-start, and shutdown;
- enable default;
- regulator stability requirements;
- rail sequencing.

### Control and safe defaults

Check:

- outputs during reset;
- enables before controller initialization;
- pullup and pulldown voltage domains;
- switches, MOSFETs, and drivers when control power is absent;
- open, shorted, or missing control signals;
- whether protection and shutdown can act independently.

### Interfaces and voltage domains

Check:

- signal direction;
- controller/peripheral or source/sink role;
- logic thresholds;
- level-shifter direction;
- pullup domain;
- bus address;
- hot-plug and disconnect state;
- ESD and misconnection path;
- exact cable and connector pins.

### Sensors and loads

Check:

- recommended external circuit;
- input, output, and load range;
- bias, gain, and filtering;
- startup and fault state;
- exposed pad;
- NC, DNC, and reserved pins;
- nodes required for calibration or prototype testing.

### Parts and packages

Check:

- whether each identity should be `exact` or `generic`;
- exact manufacturer, MPN, suffix, and function version for `exact` parts;
- value, rating, tolerance, and allowed range for `generic` parts;
- package and pin numbering;
- current purchasing identity;
- allowed substitutions.

See [1.2-parts.md](1.2-parts.md) for selection and source material.

## Run the Contract audit

```text
node <skill>/scripts/action-runner.mjs run \
  --action schematic-contract-audit \
  --input-file <schematic-contract.json>
```

The audit checks:

- JSON shape;
- component, net, and block identity;
- cross-references;
- pin-to-net ownership;
- NC and DNC state;
- power domains;
- differential and interface references;
- the basic Provider-binding boundary.

It proves only Contract structure and internal references. It does not prove circuit topology, component suitability, datasheet interpretation, EDA implementation, or physical behavior.

## Compare with the EDA schematic

After entering EDA, the Provider resolves parts, places symbols, and implements connections from the Contract.

Read back:

- actual components;
- actual pins;
- actual nets;
- power symbols;
- unconnected pins;
- critical properties;
- save state.

The Contract is intent; EDA readback is implementation. When they differ, correct the side that owns the incorrect fact rather than allowing either one to overwrite the other by default.

For EasyEDA Pro, see [2.2-schematic-workflow.md](providers/easyeda-pro/2.2-schematic-workflow.md).

## What ERC proves

ERC can find connectivity issues covered by its configured rules, such as unconnected pins, power-type conflicts, output conflicts, or missing power markers.

ERC does not decide topology, ratings, protection behavior, or system function. Explain intentional exceptions and their scope instead of hiding broad warning classes.

## Before EDA implementation

Proceed to EDA when:

- the architecture is mapped into the schematic;
- critical parts that require `exact` identity have confirmed package and pins;
- ordinary `generic` parts have complete specification and package requirements;
- rails, interfaces, and major nets are clear;
- control and protection circuits have explicit defaults;
- unconnected pins are classified;
- source facts that affect connectivity are confirmed;
- the Contract audit has no unresolved errors;
- remaining uncertainty will not change current connectivity and is expressed as a clear prototype test.

Keep working in schematic design when an open issue still changes a part, pin, power structure, or protection behavior.

## Before PCB

After EDA implementation, confirm:

- the current schematic is saved;
- actual components, pins, and nets were compared with the Contract;
- unconnected pins and intentional exceptions are explained;
- schematic DRC has no unexplained current issue;
- remaining uncertainty will not change current connectivity.

These are not prerequisites for entering EDA; they are the exit conditions after the EDA schematic exists.
